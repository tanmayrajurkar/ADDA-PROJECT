# Corporate actions and IPOs

## Dividend
A cash dividend is a distribution of profit to shareholders. The board proposes it, and a final dividend also needs shareholder approval. The record date decides who is entitled to that dividend. The ex-dividend date is the session from which the share trades without the right to it. On the ex-dividend date the price often adjusts down by about the dividend amount, all else equal, because a new buyer is not entitled to that payout. A dividend is not extra cash laid on top of an unchanged share price.

## Bonus issue
A bonus issue gives additional shares to existing holders by converting reserves, in a stated ratio such as one new share for each share held. The holder owns more shares after the bonus. The price per share adjusts so the total value of the holding does not rise merely because the share count rose. A bonus is not the same thing as a cash profit.

## Share split
A share split divides each share into shares of a smaller face value. A common example is a face value of 10 rupees becoming 1 rupee, which multiplies the share count. The market price per share adjusts in the other direction. The investor's total holding value does not increase just because there are more shares. Face value is the nominal value of the share. It is not the market price.

## Bonus versus split
A bonus issue and a split both increase the number of shares and reduce the price per share, so the arithmetic of the action itself does not create a cash profit. A bonus is funded from reserves and is announced as a ratio of new shares to old shares. A split changes the face value of each share. Investors should read the company's filing for the ratio, the record date, and the ex-date rather than treat either action as a guaranteed rise.

## Rights issue
A rights issue offers existing shareholders the chance to buy new shares, often at a discount to the recent market price, in proportion to the shares they already hold. The right may itself trade for a limited window. If a holder does nothing, their percentage ownership can fall because new shares are created. That dilution is a separate question from whether the company's use of the money is sound.

## Buyback
A buyback is a purchase by the company of its own shares, either from the market or through a tender offer, under SEBI buyback rules. Shares that are bought back are typically extinguished, which reduces the number of shares in issue. The regulations cap the size and set rules for the price and the timetable. A buyback announcement is not a guarantee that the share price will rise.

## Initial public offering
An initial public offering, or IPO, is the first sale of a company's shares to the public. The company files a draft red herring prospectus and, after SEBI's observations, a red herring prospectus. In book building, applicants bid inside a published price band. Retail applications commonly use ASBA, where the application money is blocked in the bank account, often with a UPI mandate, and is debited only if shares are allotted. Allotment can be partial when the issue is oversubscribed. Listing day is the first day of exchange trading, and the opening price can be above or below the issue price. An IPO is not a promise of listing gains.
