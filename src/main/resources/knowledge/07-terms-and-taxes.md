# Terms, charges, and tax

## Market capitalisation
Market capitalisation is the share price multiplied by the number of shares outstanding. It is the market's current value for the whole company. It is not the cash sitting in the company's bank account. Free-float market capitalisation counts only the shares that are free to trade. Large-cap, mid-cap, and small-cap are size labels used by indices and by mutual fund schemes. The rupee lines between those labels come from the published methodology and can be updated.

## Earnings, face value, and the P/E ratio
Earnings per share, or EPS, is the profit attributable to shareholders divided by the share count, for the period stated. The price-to-earnings ratio, also written P/E, is the share price divided by earnings per share. A P/E of 20 means the market price is 20 rupees for each rupee of that earnings figure. Trailing P/E uses earnings that have already been reported. Forward P/E uses estimates, and estimates can be wrong. Book value is assets minus liabilities. Face value is the nominal amount of the share, often 1, 2, 5, or 10 rupees, and it is not the trading price.

## Volume, delivery, and liquidity
Volume is the number of shares traded in a period. Delivery percentage is the part of that volume which actually moved into demat accounts instead of being closed out intraday. High volume means more activity. It does not, by itself, mean the price will keep moving the same way. A liquid stock has enough buyers and sellers that an ordinary order shifts the price only a little. An illiquid stock has a wide spread and can jump on a small order. Index constituents are generally more liquid than thinly traded smaller companies.

## Bull market, bear market, and correction
A bull market is a long stretch of rising prices and a greater willingness to take risk. A bear market is a long stretch of falling prices. A correction is an informal word for a decline that commentators often put near 10 percent from a recent high. None of these labels tells an investor the day to buy or the day to sell.

## Brokerage and statutory charges
A trade can attract brokerage set by the broker, securities transaction tax, exchange transaction charges, a SEBI turnover fee, stamp duty, and GST on some of the services. The rupee amount depends on the broker's plan, on whether the trade is delivery, intraday, or a derivative, and on the turnover. These charges are not the same thing as income tax on a profit.

## Securities transaction tax
Securities transaction tax, or STT, is collected on taxable securities transactions at rates the government notifies. Delivery trades in equity, intraday equity trades, and derivative trades do not all share one STT rate. STT is charged on the transaction itself. It is separate from the tax on capital gains.

## Capital gains on listed equity
For listed equity shares and equity-oriented mutual funds, where the STT conditions are met, the holding period chooses the kind of gain. A holding period of 12 months or less is short-term. A holding period of more than 12 months is long-term. For transfers on or after 23 July 2024, the rules described in this knowledge base tax those short-term equity gains at 20 percent, and they tax long-term equity gains at 12.5 percent on the amount above 1.25 lakh rupees in a financial year. Later Finance Acts can change rates and thresholds. This description is not a computation of anyone's tax bill.

## Dividends in the hands of the shareholder
Dividends from an Indian company are taxed in the shareholder's hands at the applicable slab. The older dividend distribution tax, which the company itself used to pay, is not the current system described here. Tax on interest from a debt instrument depends on that instrument and on the holder. This knowledge base does not calculate a personal tax liability.
