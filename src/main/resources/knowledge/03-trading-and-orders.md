# Trading, settlement, and orders

## Equity market hours
On a normal weekday, the NSE and BSE equity pre-open session starts at 9:00 IST. The continuous session runs from 9:15 IST to 15:30 IST. A short post-close session runs later in the afternoon, around 15:40 to 16:00 IST, for orders at the closing price. The exchange can change these times by circular, including on Muhurat trading day and on truncated sessions.

## Pre-open session
The pre-open session discovers an opening price after overnight news. From about 9:00 to 9:08 IST, orders are collected and are not matched continuously. The exchange then looks for a price that would match the most quantity, and that becomes the opening price. A short buffer follows, and continuous trading starts at 9:15 IST.

## T+1 settlement
Equity cash-market trades in India settle on a T+1 cycle. T is the trade date, and T+1 means securities and funds are due on the next trading day. A purchase on Monday is due to be credited to the demat account on Tuesday when Tuesday is a trading day. A sale leaves the demat account on the same cycle, and the funds are due on the next trading day. Clearing corporations and depositories carry out the settlement. A trading holiday pushes settlement to the next working day.

## Pay-in and payout
Pay-in is the step where the seller's shares and the buyer's funds move into the clearing system. Payout is the step where shares are credited toward the buyer and funds are credited toward the seller. A broker screen can show the credit in the trading ledger before the linked bank account is updated. The exchange settlement calendar, not a marketing label in an app, decides the official day.

## Trading holidays
NSE and BSE publish a holiday calendar. Weekends and declared holidays are not trading days, so they are not counted as T+1 settlement days. Muhurat trading is a special short session around Diwali. It is still a real trading session, with its own timings published by the exchange.

## Market and limit orders
A market order asks for an immediate trade at the best prices then available in the book. A large market order can fill at several prices, so the average can be worse than the last traded price. A limit order sets the highest price a buyer will pay, or the lowest price a seller will accept. A limit order can stay pending when the market never reaches that price. It protects the price and gives up the certainty of an immediate fill.

## Stop-loss orders
A stop-loss order is used to cap a loss or to protect an open gain. A stop-loss limit order, often labelled SL, turns into a limit order after the trigger price is touched. A stop-loss market order, often labelled SL-M, turns into a market order after the trigger. In a fast fall, an SL-M fill can be far from the trigger because the order takes whatever prices are still in the book.

## Order validity and product types
A day order lives only for that trading session and expires if it does not trade. An immediate-or-cancel order, called IOC, trades what it can at once and cancels the rest. Brokers also accept after-market orders that wait in a queue and go to the exchange when the next session opens. CNC and MIS are broker product codes for delivery and intraday margin. The exchange still sees a normal order in the book. Those codes are not themselves a SEBI order type.

## Delivery and intraday
A delivery trade is meant to move shares into or out of the demat account under the settlement cycle. An intraday trade is opened and closed in the same session, so there is no net share obligation when both legs actually trade. Intraday trades still carry price risk, brokerage, and securities transaction tax. If the position is left open, the broker may square it off near the close or convert it toward delivery, and the charges depend on that broker's rules.

## Bid, ask, and price bands
The bid is the best buy price standing in the order book. The ask, also called the offer, is the best sell price. The spread is the gap between the bid and the ask. A tight spread usually means the stock is liquid. Each security also trades inside a price band set by the exchange. Common bands are 2 percent, 5 percent, 10 percent, or 20 percent around a reference price, depending on the category of the stock. A stock at the upper circuit has buyers and little or no quantity offered at the limit. A stock at the lower circuit has sellers and little or no quantity bid at the limit. The exchange can revise a band. A circuit is not a promise that the price will reverse.

## Surveillance lists
Exchanges place some securities under additional surveillance measures, called ASM, or the graded surveillance measure, called GSM, when price or volume behaviour needs tighter oversight. Those lists change and are published by NSE and BSE. A stock on a surveillance list can face a tighter price band and higher margin. The list is a risk flag from the exchange. It is not a recommendation to buy or sell.
