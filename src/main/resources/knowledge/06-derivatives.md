# Derivatives

## Futures
A futures contract is an exchange-traded agreement tied to an underlying such as the Nifty 50 or a single stock, with a set price and a set expiry. Open futures positions are marked to market, so gains and losses pass through the margin account while the position is held. Index futures are cash settled. Stock futures can be physically settled if they are left open into expiry, which means shares may have to be delivered under the exchange rules. The lot size is fixed by the exchange and is revised from time to time. The futures price can sit away from the spot price because of interest, dividends, and the demand to hold that contract.

## Options
A call option is the right, but not the obligation, to buy the underlying at the strike price. A put option is the right, but not the obligation, to sell at the strike price. The buyer pays a premium. The buyer's largest loss is that premium. The seller of an option takes on a much larger risk and must keep margin with the broker. Options on the Nifty 50, Nifty Bank, and single stocks trade on the exchange. Expiry dates follow the schedule in exchange circulars, and that weekday has been changed before, so the current expiry should be read from the exchange rather than remembered.

## Margin and leverage
Derivative positions require margin, collected under the clearing corporation's margin framework, including SPAN and exposure margin or the successor method the clearing corporation publishes. Margin is a deposit against possible loss. It is not a fee that buys the contract. If losses pull the balance below the requirement, the broker raises a margin call. If funds are not added, the position can be squared off. Leverage means a small move in the underlying is a large percentage gain or loss on the margin that was posted.

## Why futures and options are used
Hedgers use futures and options to reduce a risk they already hold, for example an equity portfolio against a fall in the Nifty 50. Speculators use them to take a view on direction or on volatility. Neither use removes the chance of losing money. An option premium depends on the spot price, the strike, the time left to expiry, volatility, and interest rates. A small premium in rupees can still be expensive relative to the risk it covers. It is not cheap merely because the number looks small.
