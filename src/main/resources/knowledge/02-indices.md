# Indices

## Nifty 50
The Nifty 50 is the benchmark index of the National Stock Exchange. It represents 50 large and liquid companies listed on the NSE, spread across major sectors of the Indian economy. The index is weighted by free-float market capitalisation. Free float means shares that are available for public trading, leaving out promoter holdings and other shares that are not freely traded. The base date of the Nifty 50 is 3 November 1995 and the base value is 1,000. A committee reviews the constituents. Membership changes when a company no longer fits the size or liquidity rules, or when another company qualifies.

## Sensex
The S&P BSE Sensex is the benchmark index of the BSE. Sensex is short for Sensitive Index. It tracks 30 large and established companies listed on the BSE. The Sensex is free-float market-capitalisation weighted. Its base period is 1978-79 and its base value is 100. Because it holds fewer stocks than the Nifty 50, one heavy constituent can move the Sensex by more points than it moves the Nifty 50.

## Other Indian indices
Nifty Bank tracks large banks listed on the NSE and is a common underlying for index derivatives. Nifty IT tracks major information-technology companies. Nifty Next 50 covers the next 50 companies after the Nifty 50 under the index rules. Nifty Midcap and Nifty Smallcap series track smaller companies. Broader baskets such as the Nifty 500 and the BSE 500 spread across large, mid-size, and smaller names. A sector index can rise or fall on its own even when the Nifty 50 is flat.

## How to read an index level
An index level is not the rupee price of a share. It is a number that shows how a basket has moved compared with its base. If the Nifty 50 moves from 20,000 to 22,000, the basket is 10 percent higher than it was at 20,000. Commentators report the change both in points and in percent. An investor cannot buy the index number directly. The usual routes are an index fund, an exchange-traded fund, or index futures and options.

## Index funds and ETFs
An index fund is a mutual fund that tries to copy an index such as the Nifty 50. An exchange-traded fund, or ETF, also tracks an index, but it is bought and sold on the exchange during market hours like a share. Both can drift slightly from the index because of costs and tracking error. The expense ratio is the annual cost charged by the fund. A lower expense ratio is a fact about cost, not a forecast of higher returns.

## Market-wide circuit breakers
Market-wide circuit breakers are tied to large moves in the Nifty 50 or the Sensex. At 10 percent, 15 percent, and 20 percent from the previous close, trading can be halted. The length of the halt depends on which level was hit and the time of day. These breakers are meant to slow a panic. They do not predict the next move. Individual stocks also have their own price bands, which are separate from a market-wide halt.
