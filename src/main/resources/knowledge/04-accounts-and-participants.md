# Accounts and participants

## Trading, bank, and demat accounts
An investor who wants to buy and sell shares typically needs three linked accounts. The bank account holds rupees. The trading account, opened with a SEBI-registered broker, is used to place orders. The demat account holds the securities in electronic form. Many brokers package the three as one onboarding. The broker does not replace the depository. Shares are credited at NSDL or CDSL through a depository participant.

## Demat account
Demat is short for dematerialised. Shares sit as electronic entries instead of paper certificates. A depository participant, often the broker or a bank, opens the demat account for the investor. India has two depositories: NSDL, the National Securities Depository Limited, which began operations in 1996, and CDSL, Central Depository Services Limited, which began in 1999. The legal base is the Depositories Act, 1996. Each investor receives a demat account number, sometimes called a BO ID. Each security is identified by an ISIN, the International Securities Identification Number.

## KYC
Opening a trading account and a demat account requires KYC. The investor provides PAN, proof of identity and address, a photograph, and bank details. SEBI's KYC rules allow the record to be stored with a KYC Registration Agency so a later intermediary can reuse it. Accounts for a minor, a joint holding, or an NRI need extra documents. An investor should open these accounts only with a SEBI-registered intermediary.

## How a purchase is recorded
When a purchase settles, the clearing corporation and the depository tell the depository participant to credit that ISIN quantity to the buyer's demat account. The broker app may show the shares earlier as a pending credit. The depository statement, or the consolidated account statement called CAS, is the holding record. CAS is sent by the depositories and can include both demat securities and mutual fund units.

## Retail investors
Retail investors are individuals who trade their own money and fall under the size limits the rules use for a retail category. They place orders through a broker's app or terminal. Retail buying or selling does not, by itself, set the fair value of a company.

## Domestic institutions
Domestic institutional investors, called DIIs, include Indian mutual funds, insurance companies, banks, and other local institutions. A mutual fund pools money from many people and invests it under a scheme document. The NAV is the per-unit value of that scheme after expenses. NAV is not the same number as a company's share price.

## Foreign portfolio investors
Foreign portfolio investors, called FPIs, are overseas institutions registered to invest in Indian securities. Market commentary still uses the older name FII, for foreign institutional investor. SEBI supervises FPI registration and the applicable investment limits. Their trades can move liquid stocks. A single day's FPI flow number is not an instruction to buy or sell.

## Brokers, promoters, and public shareholders
A stockbroker is a trading member of an exchange. Only a registered broker can send an order into that exchange. A clearing member is responsible for settlement obligations with the clearing corporation, and many large brokers do both jobs. The broker is an intermediary. The broker is not the exchange and is not SEBI. Promoters are the controlling shareholders named in the company's disclosures. Public shareholders are the rest. Free-float market capitalisation, which index weights use, focuses on the public portion. A promoter pledge means those shares have been given as collateral. A high pledge is a disclosed risk. It is not automatically a reason to buy or sell.
